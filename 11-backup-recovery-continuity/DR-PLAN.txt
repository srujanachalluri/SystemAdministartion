----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 1 - WHAT WE ARE PROTECTING AND WHY IT MATTERS
----------------------------------------------------------------

Grace Fellowship runs a RAG assistant that answers questions about
ministries, events, and the church's positions. It runs on a
fine-tuned open-weight model, a pgvector store holding embeddings
and source documents, a prompt library the volunteer team tuned
over dozens of revisions, and a small hand-labelled evaluation set.

The scenario this plan is written against: a volunteer's laptop is
compromised by phishing on a Friday. By Monday, ransomware has
encrypted the serving host AND the one external drive somebody had
been manually copying "the AI files" onto - because that drive was
mounted and writable at the time.

That last clause is the whole lesson. A copy that is online and
writable when the attack happens is not a backup. It is a second
victim.

So the two decisions that drive this entire plan are:

  1. Which assets can we NOT re-create? Those get protected.
  2. Which copies can ransomware reach? Those need to be
     immutable, not just remote.


----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2 - CLASSIFICATION: WHAT IS IRREPLACEABLE, WHAT IS NOT
----------------------------------------------------------------

The full register with per-asset reasoning is in
dr-asset-register.yaml. Summary of the calls I made:

IRREPLACEABLE - we back these up

  fine-tuned-weights
    11.5 GPU-hours of training on a corpus that has since been
    edited. Even with identical data the run is not bit-for-bit
    reproducible. This is the one people assume is replaceable
    because "we can just retrain" - you cannot, because the exact
    training corpus no longer exists in that form.

  vector-db-source
    The documents and their embeddings. Three months of volunteer
    curation and labelling. If this goes, the project effectively
    ends, because nobody is volunteering three more months.

  prompt-library
    Tiny in bytes, large in behaviour. Forty-plus revisions is
    what stops the assistant inventing service times and answering
    doctrinal questions it should be refusing.

  app-configs
    Retrieval thresholds, rerank depth, refusal rules. Tuned
    empirically. Nobody remembers why min_score is 0.62, which is
    exactly why it has to be archived. Secrets are NOT included -
    only the .env template. See section 7.

  eval-datasets
    Hand-labelled question and answer pairs. Without them we
    cannot tell whether a restored model is the model we had.

REPRODUCIBLE - we deliberately do NOT back these up

  vector-db-hnsw-index
    A graph derived entirely from embeddings we already protect.
    Storing it would mean paying for bytes we can regenerate.
    We store the CREATE INDEX statement instead.

  base-model-checkpoint
    4.7 GB of open-weight bytes anyone can re-pull. We store the
    DIGEST, not the bytes, because the digest is what a recovery
    actually needs. Never ":latest" - a tag that moves is not a
    recovery point.

The one that is easy to get wrong: reproducible does not mean
unimportant. Both reproducible assets are on the critical path.
The assistant cannot serve anybody without the base model, and
without the HNSW index retrieval degrades to a sequential scan and
the assistant is too slow to use. We do not store them; we do
budget recovery time for them. Those are different decisions and
conflating them is how RTO estimates end up wrong.

Evidence that we really did skip them, rather than forgetting:
backup_ai_estate.sh writes output/reproducible-receipt-<stamp>.txt
on every run, recording how many bytes were skipped and the exact
command to rebuild each one.

SECTION 3 - THE 3-2-1 DESIGN, AND WHICH LOCK MODE WHERE
----------------------------------------------------------------

3 copies:
  1. the live estate on the serving host
  2. a local archive folder, written on every backup run
  3. object storage, object-locked, treated as offsite

2 media:
  local filesystem, and object storage

1 offsite:
  the object-locked buckets. In production these are S3 in a
  different region than the serving host. In this lab they are
  MinIO on localhost, which speaks the same S3 API, so the object
  lock behaviour being tested is real and not simulated.

HONEST GAP: in this lab all three copies sit on one disk, because
a Codespace has one disk. The 3-2-1 topology is implemented and
every part of it is tested, but the physical separation is not
real here. The production mapping is: copy 2 to the church NAS,
copy 3 to S3 in a second region. I am naming this rather than
pretending the lab satisfies it, because a plan that overstates
its own coverage is worse than one with a documented gap.

WHY TWO BUCKETS INSTEAD OF ONE

Object Lock has two modes and they are not interchangeable.

  gf-ai-worm   COMPLIANCE mode, 30-day retention
               fine-tuned-weights, eval-datasets

    COMPLIANCE means no version can be deleted before its
    retention expires. Not by an ordinary user, not with a bypass
    flag, not with the root credential. These two assets never
    legitimately change after a release, so the only thing that
    would ever want to overwrite them is ransomware. Giving up our
    own ability to delete them costs us nothing we want.

  gf-ai-gov    GOVERNANCE mode, 30-day retention
               vector-db-source, prompt-library, app-configs

    GOVERNANCE refuses ordinary deletes but allows a privileged
    caller passing --bypass to delete a version on purpose. We
    keep that escape hatch for two specific, foreseeable events:

      - A member asks us to delete a document about them. That
        request has to be honourable inside the backups too, not
        just in the live database.
      - A volunteer pastes a real credential into a prompt file
        and it gets archived. Under COMPLIANCE that leak would be
        permanent for the full retention window.

    The cost of that hatch, stated plainly: an attacker who gets
    a sufficiently privileged credential could use --bypass too.
    That is precisely why the weights and eval sets are NOT in
    this bucket. The tier that needs no escape hatch does not get
    one.

The chapter hint says object lock cuts both ways, and it does. The
mode is chosen per tier based on whether we can name a legitimate
reason to ever delete. If we can, GOVERNANCE. If we cannot,
COMPLIANCE.

ONE-WAY DOOR: Object Lock requires versioning and can only be
enabled when a bucket is CREATED. You cannot add it to a bucket
you already have. That is why setup_estate.sh uses
"mc mb --with-lock" and why the two-bucket split had to be decided
up front rather than adjusted later.

Both modes were tested rather than assumed. See
prove_immutability.sh and section 6.

SECTION 4 - THE RUNBOOK
----------------------------------------------------------------

Written to be followed at 2 a.m. by somebody who is not thinking
clearly. Do the steps in order. Do not skip step 0.

STEP 0 - STOP AND CONTAIN (before restoring anything)

  0.1  Disconnect the serving host from the network. Do not
       reboot it - memory may matter for the investigation.
  0.2  Do NOT mount any backup target as writable from the
       compromised host. This is how the external drive was lost
       in the first place. Restore onto a CLEAN host.
  0.3  Rotate every credential in the password manager: the
       Postgres password, the object storage keys, and any API
       keys in the serving config. Assume all of them are known
       to the attacker.
  0.4  Check the object storage audit log for delete or bypass
       attempts. Under COMPLIANCE they will have failed, but the
       attempts tell you what the attacker was after.

  Restoring onto a still-compromised host just re-encrypts the
  restore. Contain first.

STEP 1 - CONFIRM WE HAVE SOMETHING TO RESTORE (target: 10 min)

  source code/env.sh
  mc ls -r gf/gf-ai-worm
  mc ls -r gf/gf-ai-gov
  mc retention info --default gf/gf-ai-worm

  Pick the most recent stamp from before the compromise date.
  If the newest objects are dated after the compromise, use
  "mc ls --versions" and choose an earlier version. Object Lock
  means the older versions are still there.

STEP 2 - RESTORE INTO SCRATCH AND VERIFY, NOT INTO PRODUCTION

  ./code/restore_test.sh <STAMP>

  Never restore straight into production. This script restores
  into a temporary folder and a throwaway database, verifies
  checksums, counts rows, and rebuilds the index. If it fails
  here, you have learned that for free. If it passes, you now
  know the archive is good before you touch anything live.

STEP 3 - RECOVER THE ASSETS, IN THIS ORDER

  Order matters. It follows the critical path.

  3.1  base model      (60 min - the longest step, start it first)
         ollama pull llama3.1:8b-instruct-q4_K_M
         Verify the digest against models/base/PIN.txt.
         If a digest-pinned local cache exists, copy from it
         instead. See section 5.

  3.2  fine-tuned weights                            (45 min)
         mc cp -r gf/gf-ai-worm/models/finetuned/ ./
         Verify against the sha256 manifest BEFORE loading it.

  3.3  vector source                                 (30 min)
         mc cp gf/gf-ai-gov/vectors/vectors-<STAMP>.dump .
         pg_restore --section=pre-data --section=data \
                    --dbname=gf_ragdb vectors-<STAMP>.dump
         Check the row count against what the register expects.

  3.4  HNSW index - REBUILD, do not restore    (measured, sec 5)
         CREATE INDEX documents_embedding_hnsw ON documents
           USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops)
           WITH (m = 16, ef_construction = 64);

  3.5  prompt library                                (10 min)
         mc cp gf/gf-ai-gov/prompts/prompts-<STAMP>.bundle .
         git clone prompts-<STAMP>.bundle prompts

  3.6  configs                                       (10 min)
         mc cp gf/gf-ai-gov/configs/configs-<STAMP>.tgz .
         tar -xzf configs-<STAMP>.tgz
         Then re-enter the real secrets from the password manager.
         They are NOT in this archive, on purpose. See section 7.

STEP 4 - PROVE IT ACTUALLY WORKS BEFORE ANNOUNCING RECOVERY

  4.1  Run the eval set from
       gf-ai-worm/datasets/eval_qa.jsonl. Compare against the
       recorded expected answers.
  4.2  Confirm the refusal cases still refuse. A restored model
       that has lost its refusal behaviour is a liability, not a
       recovery.
  4.3  Only after 4.1 and 4.2 pass, put it back in front of
       congregants.

STEP 5 - AFTER

  Write down what actually broke and what the real timings were.
  Update the rto_minutes in dr-asset-register.yaml with measured
  numbers. Update last_restore_test. Run rpo_rto.py again.


----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 5 - THE NUMBERS, AND THE WEAKEST LINK
----------------------------------------------------------------

These are computed by rpo_rto.py from the register, not guessed.
The committed output is in output/rpo-rto-output.txt.

RECOVERY POINT OBJECTIVE

  worst case, any asset  : 1440 min (24 hours)
  service critical       :   60 min (1 hour)

  I report two numbers on purpose. A single figure of 1440 minutes
  reads like negligence. The two assets carrying it - the weights
  and the eval sets - only change during a planned retrain, which
  is an announced event, so a daily snapshot loses nothing in
  practice. What a congregant would actually notice losing is
  newly added content, and that is protected hourly.

RECOVERY TIME OBJECTIVE, serial recovery

  chapter formula        :  125 min
  true critical path     :  175 min   <-- the number I will defend

  The chapter's formula sums the irreplaceable assets plus
  anything marked rebuild-on-recovery. That scores the base model
  checkpoint at zero minutes, because it is reproducible with a
  cache-not-backup strategy. But the assistant cannot answer a
  single question until those 4.7 GB are back on disk. Fifty
  minutes of real downtime were missing from the reported figure.

  I added a critical_path flag to the register and made rpo_rto.py
  sum that instead. It prints both numbers so the difference is
  visible rather than quietly corrected. I am reporting the worse
  number because it is the true one.

WEAKEST LINK ON THE CRITICAL PATH

  base-model-checkpoint - 60 minutes, 34% of total RTO.

  The single largest block of downtime in this estate is not any
  of the irreplaceable assets. It is re-downloading a file that is
  freely available and that we deliberately chose not to store.

  That is a genuinely uncomfortable finding, and it is the most
  useful thing in this plan. The fix is cheap and it is not "back
  up the base model": keep a digest-pinned local cache on the
  recovery host, so step 3.1 becomes a local copy instead of a
  download. That would cut the estate RTO from 175 minutes to
  roughly 120. Recommended as the next action.

PREDICTED VERSUS MEASURED INDEX REBUILD

  predicted (in the register) : 20 minutes
  measured (restore_test.sh)  : FILL IN FROM output/restore-test.log

  This is the honest-gap line the chapter asks for. Fill in the
  measured number from your restore test run, then write one
  sentence here about the gap.

  For context on how to read your number: the lab has 512 chunks.
  Grace Fellowship's real corpus is closer to 180,000 chunks. HNSW
  build time grows roughly with n log n, so a lab measurement of
  well under a second does NOT mean production rebuilds in under a
  second. Scaling 512 to 180,000 is about a 350x increase in rows
  and roughly 500x in work, so a 0.6 second lab build implies
  something in the region of 5 minutes at production scale.

  Which means my 20 minute prediction was probably pessimistic by
  about 4x. That is still the right direction to be wrong in for a
  DR estimate, but it is worth correcting, because a padded RTO on
  one asset hides where the real time goes - and in this estate
  the real time is the base model download, not the index.

SECTION 6 - HOW WE KNOW ANY OF THIS IS TRUE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Nothing in this plan is asserted without a test.

  Restore works
    restore_test.sh, transcript in output/restore-test.log.
    Verifies dataset checksums against the manifest captured at
    backup time, verifies the weights checksums, clones the prompt
    bundle, restores the vector dump into a throwaway database,
    compares the recovered row count against production, confirms
    the embedding dimensions survived, confirms the derived index
    did NOT come back from the archive, rebuilds it, times the
    rebuild, and runs a similarity query against the result.

  The object lock is real
    prove_immutability.sh, transcript in
    output/immutability-test.log. It attacks a canary object in
    each bucket and checks that the right calls fail.

  We did not pay to store derived state
    output/reproducible-receipt-<stamp>.txt, written on every
    backup run, plus the explicit check in restore_test.sh step 4
    that zero HNSW indexes came back from the archive.

  The numbers are computed
    output/rpo-rto-output.txt, produced by rpo_rto.py from the
    register.

TWO THINGS THAT WENT WRONG WHILE BUILDING THIS

Both are written up in agent-log.txt. Recording them here too
because both changed the design.

  1. The first immutability test was WRONG. It ran "mc rm" on a
     locked object and asserted it would fail. It did not fail -
     it reported success. The near-miss conclusion was "object
     lock is broken." It is not. On a versioned bucket, rm with no
     version id writes a DELETE MARKER: the object disappears from
     a normal listing while the locked version sits underneath,
     untouched and still readable. Object Lock protects VERSIONS,
     not names. The corrected test deletes a specific version id,
     which is what an attacker would actually have to do.

     Operational consequence, in the runbook at step 1: if the
     newest object looks wrong or missing, use "mc ls --versions"
     and recover the earlier version. A file that looks deleted
     probably is not.

  2. The first version of that test attacked the real weights
     object instead of a canary. It left a 24-byte "ransomware"
     file as the current version and broke the next restore test -
     and because the bucket is COMPLIANCE locked, the mess could
     not be deleted. That is a fast and memorable way to learn
     what COMPLIANCE mode actually means. Tests now run against a
     disposable canary.


----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 7 - THINGS DELIBERATELY LEFT OUT, AND KNOWN GAPS
----------------------------------------------------------------

SECRETS ARE NOT IN THE BACKUP

  Only configs/.env.template is archived. Real credentials live in
  the password manager. This is a decision, not an oversight: an
  archive full of live credentials is a second breach waiting to
  happen, and it is one an attacker gets for free once they reach
  the archive. The cost is that step 3.6 of the runbook requires a
  human with password manager access. That is the right trade.

KNOWN GAPS, STATED PLAINLY

  1. All three copies are on one disk in this lab. Discussed in
     section 3. Production mapping is documented; the physical
     separation is not tested here.

  2. The full runbook has never been executed end to end on a
     genuinely clean host. restore_test.sh proves the archive is
     good and restorable. It does not prove that step 0
     containment works under real pressure, and it does not prove
     the 175 minute figure - that is a sum of per-asset estimates,
     only one of which has been measured. A full drill on a clean
     host is the second recommended action after the base model
     cache.

  3. Retention is 30 days. If a compromise is not noticed for
     more than 30 days, the clean versions may have expired. For a
     volunteer-run estate where nobody is watching daily, 30 days
     is arguably too short. Worth revisiting at the next quarterly
     review.

  4. Only one restore test has been run. A single pass proves the
     archive is restorable today. It does not prove the backup
     stays restorable as the estate changes shape. The review
     cadence exists for that reason and the last_restore_test
     field is there to make a stale plan visible.


----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 8 - RECOMMENDATION
----------------------------------------------------------------

The architecture decision I would put my name on:

Keep the two-tier lock split, and spend the next hour of effort on
a digest-pinned local base-model cache rather than on any of the
irreplaceable assets.

The reasoning: the irreplaceable tier is already protected about
as well as this estate can afford. It is checksummed, it is
COMPLIANCE locked, and the restore has been measured rather than
assumed. Adding more protection there has diminishing returns.

Meanwhile 34% of total recovery time - the single largest block -
is spent re-downloading a file that is freely available and that
we correctly chose not to back up. The classification is right.
The recovery design around it is not. Caching it, pinned by
digest, cuts about 55 minutes off the estate RTO for the cost of
4.7 GB of disk.

That is the call an agent could not have made for me, because it
depends on knowing that this is a church running on volunteer
internet where a 4.7 GB download is slow, and on having noticed
that the chapter's own RTO formula scores that asset at zero.
